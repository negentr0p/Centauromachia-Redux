Abstract

In Philip K. Dick’s 1977 essay “If You Find This World Bad,” he envisions a painting that continually transforms itself—not through linear replacement but lateral shifts within the same canvas. “Centauromachia Redux” realizes this metaphor through an AI illustration model, trained on the artist’s style, generating perpetual renditions of Renaissance centauromachia. The project explores Kasparov’s centaur chess paradigm of human-AI collaboration, positioning centaurs as civilized entities where technology constitutes humanity itself, questioning who represents civilization amid destabilized human-machine hierarchies. Framed as a “Metanoia,” the project draws on Jung’s personal metamorphosis to integrate AI critically within practice, producing a new working mythology as the illustrator curates machinic style transfer.

About Negentrop

Negentrop is an experimental illustration project, an attempt at a new way of working. Illustration has long illuminated texts; with the image now in the ascendant, Negentrop illustrates the apparatus of image-making from within, and its methodology is negentropy: beginning again from chaotic image overload and reordering it to find new possibilities.

It belongs to a lineage of experimental attempts to short-circuit authorship, from Eduardo Paolozzi’s Bunk lecture (1952) to Walter Benjamin’s unfinished Arcades Project, which quotes without quotation marks. Negentrop has no end point and no named author.

It responds to a contemporary mode of cybernetic and eschatological thinking, treating religion as a technological construct and eschatology as a technique that can be re-engineered. Where the eschatological urge anticipates the end of the world or of art, Negentrop turns it on the myth of the individual creator, which has to go before work can begin again. The result is a negentropic eschatology, aware of the contradiction in the term.

All images it produces are released under CC0, with their provenance documented. Any talk about creativity, it contends, must also address the end of ownership.

How the images are made

Every image on this site was generated with a diffusion model: Stable Diffusion XL 1.0 base plus the NGNTRP LoRA, trained on original paintings by Negentrop. From LoRA v3 onwards a small set of public-domain and CC0 images from the PD12M dataset is used alongside the paintings, as regularisation. Each image’s details list exactly what its LoRA version was trained on. The SDXL base model’s own training data is not publicly itemised.

Open any image in the gallery to see its prompt, seed, settings (including the LoRA strength, which is how strongly the NGNTRP style is applied) and model versions. The same note is embedded in the image file, marking it as AI-generated, although some platforms remove embedded metadata when images are shared.

Every image is reviewed by the artist before it is published.
